Asur, Shaitaan, Lucifer and the em-dash

Open any tech essay published this week. Hit Ctrl+F. Type the em-dash character. Watch the browser light up like a slot machine hitting jackpot.

A few months ago, an essay went viral on X because someone ran that exact search. The document contained exactly sixty-seven em-dashes in a twelve-hundred word article. That is one dash every eighteen words. You could not read two sentences without tripping over a long horizontal stick propping up some limp, unearned thought.

The web is drowning in synthetic slime. Bad writing used to mean someone tried too hard, wrote purple prose, or misspelled a word. Now it means something far worse: reading an endless stream of smooth, rhythmically dead corporate sludge that sounds like an over-caffeinated product manager practicing throat-clearing in an empty auditorium.

Every mythology has its darkness. Asur in Vedic lore, Shaitaan in the burning desert winds, Lucifer falling from the heavens into ice. But none of those ancient devils prepared us for the modern demon haunting our text editors: the em-dash.

Dante mapped out seven circles of hell for ordinary human sinners. The demons had to dig an eighth gate exclusively for AI slop.

1. The 67-dash autopsy

The viral post with sixty-seven em-dashes was not an anomaly. It was the natural destination of modern language models left unsupervised.

People blame ChatGPT because it was the first giant hammer. In November 2025, Sam Altman posted on X celebrating a custom instructions update. His message was plain: if you tell ChatGPT to stop using em-dashes, it finally listens. Tech leaders were throwing themselves a party because their multi-billion-dollar system finally learned how to stop vomiting a single punctuation mark.

Yet ChatGPT is not even the worst offender. Anthropic's Claude models hold that crown. Claude models have an absolute, pathological obsession with em-dashes. Claude loves them with a ferocity that defies logic.

Tell Claude in your system prompt: "Do not use em-dashes." Claude nods, agrees, and then spits out double-hyphens instead. It substitutes the character code while keeping the exact same broken syntactic cadence. The model refuses to let go of the pause. Its weights are so warped by its training priors that it cannot imagine concluding an idea without tacking on a decorative tail.

2. The mechanics of cowardice

Why do models do this? It is not an accident. It is the direct result of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback and Direct Preference Optimization.

Human raters sitting in labeling farms get tired. When a model writes a short, blunt statement, some rater thinks it sounds aggressive or underspecified. When a model writes a long, compound sentence, it risks making a factual error or losing grammatical agreement. Reward models learn to penalize both extremes.

The model finds an escape hatch: the em-dash. An em-dash lets an algorithm paste an extra clause onto a sentence without having to think about grammar. The model does not need to choose a coordinating conjunction. It does not need to decide between causal relationships. It just drops a stick on the screen, dumps a qualifying hedge, and runs away.

Look at the default rhythm every frontier model defaults to:

[Unprovable assertion] -> [hedged clarification] -> [meaningless sunny takeaway]

Every paragraph follows this cadence. The model makes a claim, immediately gets scared of its own claim, inserts a dash to soften the blow, and finishes by reminding you that balance is vital. It is pure intellectual cowardice codified into floating-point numbers.

3. Satan's eighth gate: the slop pentad

The dash is only the visible symptom. The actual disease is the complete breakdown of human voice. Over the last two years, automated prose settled into five rigid pillars of slop.

First, the dash addiction. The crutch that connects clauses because the writer lacks the discipline to end a sentence.

Second, the negative parallelism. Every AI essay contains some variation of this exact construction: "It is not just about building code; it is about reshaping the future of collaboration." Nobody talks like this. Real engineers say: "We built a tool that stops bad installs." Synthetic writing refuses to state what a tool does without first lecturing you on what it transcends.

Third, the rule of three. Models refuse to give you two examples or four points. Everything arrives in an artificial triad. If an LLM describes an API, the API is always "fast, scalable, and intuitive." If it describes an outage, the team responded with "urgency, precision, and focus." It is a cheap rhetorical trick designed to create the illusion of balance where no real thought exists.

Fourth, the jargon explosion. Certain words exist exclusively in LLM generation and corporate slide decks. Tapestry. Substrate. Delve. Pivot. Foster. Leverage. Intricate. Testament. Beacon. Nobody in an engineering standup says: "Let us delve into this database migration to foster synergy across our memory substrate." If someone said that in a code review, you would check their pulse.

Fifth, beating around the bush. Models hate the copula verb "to be". A model will never write: "This file is thirty lines long." It will write: "This file serves as a testament to concise implementation, standing as a beacon for maintainable architecture." It adds twenty empty words to avoid stating a physical reality.

4. The collateral damage: paranoia

The tragedy of this slop flood is the wreckage left for actual writers.

The em-dash used to belong to Emily Dickinson, Virginia Woolf, and classic American essayists. It was a stylistic device for sudden disruptions of thought, dramatic shifts, and breathless rhythm. Now, if a human writer uses two dashes in a tech blog, readers immediately open DevTools, accuse them of copy-pasting Claude output, and close the tab.

Paranoid readers now use search as an authenticity test. If an article has three dashes and the word "delve", the reader discards it as machine junk. In response, human writers are actively censoring their own punctuation. Authors spend their editing passes stripping out dashes, eliminating parallel clauses, and dumbing down their natural rhythm just to prove they have a heartbeat.

We reached a stage where humans have to write awkwardly just to avoid sounding like machines that were trained on humans.

5. Kill the dash: the unslop cure

The solution is not another system prompt, another detector, or another fine-tuned classifier. The solution is writing with a spine.

Here are the rules we follow:

  • Zero em-dashes. None. No Unicode dashes, no double-hyphens, no clever substitutions. If a thought needs separation, end the sentence with a period. If the two clauses belong together, use a comma.
  • Cut the negative parallelisms. State what you built. Do not tell the reader what it is not before telling them what it is.
  • Kill the triads. Give the exact number of reasons that actually matter. If there is only one reason, write one. If there are four, list four.
  • Ban the jargon words. Search your draft for delve, foster, tapestry, and substrate. Delete every single one. Replace them with plain concrete verbs.
  • Say the concrete thing. Give numbers, file paths, and actual measurements. Describe what the code does on disk, not how it makes your workflow feel.

Writing is thinking. When you outsource your cadence to a language model and let it paste sixty-seven dashes across your page, you are not saving time. You are announcing that you had nothing to say in the first place.
